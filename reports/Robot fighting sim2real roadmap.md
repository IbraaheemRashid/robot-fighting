# Train small fighting bots in simulation first

Yes: Big Hero 6-style bot fights, with small robots piloted by people, can be built simulation-first and transferred to real hardware. Hobbyists and small teams can do it as of late 2026, but only if they copy how real events work rather than how the film works. Every public humanoid fight so far has used **human pilots on joysticks, VR, or mocap suits**, with a learned onboard controller keeping the robot balanced and playing back fight moves learned from mocap. Learned autonomous boxing has only just reached hardware, and only qualitatively: RoboStriker, on two Unitree G1s, trained on a **single RTX 4090** ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.16195)). The full recipe is open and runs on one GPU: retarget human mocap, train a whole-body tracker in MuJoCo or Isaac Lab, compress the skills into a latent space, then run self-play in that space. The binding constraint is hardware. Any fight needs **two robots**, and the cheapest open humanoids with working sim2real pipelines cost about **$5-6k each** (ToddlerBot, Berkeley Humanoid Lite). For the film's mind-reading headset, non-invasive EEG delivers about one command every 4-5 seconds. Wrist EMG or a gamepad is the realistic "neural" interface. A sensible path starts with a $300-450 wheeled insect-class bot in simulation and moves to small legged or humanoid robots only once the pipeline works. Keep humans out of the ring: California has already stopped human-vs-robot bouts.

## Every real robot fight so far has had a human pilot

The commercial version of Big Hero 6 already exists, and it is person-controlled. China's CMG Mecha Fighting Series (Hangzhou, May 2025) pitted four **human-operated Unitree G1s** against each other over three 2-minute rounds. A fall cost 5 points, and staying down past 8 seconds cost 10 more ([Henan gov / Xinhua](https://english.henan.gov.cn/2025/05-26/3162232.html)). Accounts of the control setup disagree. Sources describe joysticks ([Asia Times](https://asiatimes.com/?p=920009)), remote devices plus voice commands ([eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/news/unitree-robot-combat-2026-apac-china/)), and motion sensors ([Ziegler](https://ziegler.substack.com/p/ep64-worlds-first-humanoid-robot)). All agree that Unitree captured kickboxers' movements and trained them in simulation ([Interesting Engineering](https://interestingengineering.com/innovation/unitree-plans-robot-boxing-match)). In San Francisco, REK runs **VR-piloted modified G1s** in bouts of five 60-second rounds, with tickets at $60-80. A 13-year-old pilot won a bout, and robots "sometimes fell and needed human help to stand back up" ([Rest of World](https://restofworld.org/2026/chinese-robot-boxing-unitree-rek/)). EngineAI's URKL put 32 university and industry teams on standardized T800s, with a ¥10M prize pool. One robot had its **head kicked off** and kept fighting ([PC Guide](https://www.pcguide.com/pro/news-pro/chinas-engineai-hosts-worlds-first-robot-mma-competition-t800-loses-head-and-keeps-on-fighting/)). UC Berkeley's Ken Goldberg calls much of this "robot theater" ([Rest of World](https://restofworld.org/2026/chinese-robot-boxing-unitree-rek/)).

The hobby precedent is older. **ROBO-ONE** has run remote-controlled RC-servo humanoid fights in Japan since 2002. Robots must pass pre-screening by walking 10 steps in 10 seconds and getting up from both front and back. Bouts are won by knockdowns or ring-outs, weight classes date from 2007, and an autonomous division was added in 2017 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robo_One)). Twenty years of kit robots such as the Kondo KHR-3HV show that small servo humanoids survive repeated falls. ROBO-ONE's rules are a ready-made template for a small-bot league.

On the research side, learned competitive agents have a long simulation history. OpenAI's 2017 sumo humanoids learned blocking, ducking, and tackling from self-play ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.03748)). Won, Gopinath and Hodgins (2021) trained simulated characters to box and fence by imitating basic skills first and then learning bout strategy with RL ([Meta AI](https://ai.meta.com/research/publications/control-strategies-for-physically-simulated-characters-performing-two-player-competitive-sports/)). The decisive hardware result is DeepMind's **Robotis OP3 1v1 soccer** (Science Robotics, 2024). Trained in MuJoCo and transferred zero-shot, the small humanoids walked **181% faster** than a scripted baseline, turned 302% faster, and **got up 63% faster** ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13653)). The authors found the sim2real gap "surprisingly low" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.13653v2)). That robot is the closest analogue to a small fighting bot, and it shows that learned get-up skills transfer to cheap, light hardware.

### RoboStriker brings learned boxing to real robots, under two arXiv IDs

The first autonomous humanoid boxing with sim-to-real transfer is **RoboStriker**, and the research notes cite it under two arXiv identifiers that this report cannot fully reconcile. **arXiv 2601.22517** (30 January 2026), "RoboStriker: Hierarchical Decision-Making for Autonomous Humanoid Boxing," lists ten authors (Yin, Cao, Dong and others). It describes the three-stage pipeline and claims sim-to-real transfer in its abstract ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.22517)). **arXiv 2608.16195** (17 August 2026) appears in one note as a fetched arXiv HTML page attributed to Shanghai AI Lab and SJTU, with full experimental detail ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.16195)). An aggregator titles that version "Latent-Space Strategic Games for Autonomous Humanoid Boxing" ([roboticscenter.ai](https://www.roboticscenter.ai/research/papers/robostriker-latent-space-strategic-games-for-autonomous-humanoid-boxing-2608)). A revision of the same arXiv paper keeps its original ID, so two IDs mean two separate submissions. The likeliest reading is a January preprint followed by an expanded or retitled August paper from overlapping authors, but the notes do not compare author lists, and the aggregator listing was not checked against arXiv. Readers should treat the January ID as the original method description and the August ID as the source of the detailed numbers below, and should check both pages before citing either.

The August paper's method is the template for a learned fighter. It runs in **Isaac Lab** at 200 Hz physics with a 50 Hz policy, using a 29-DoF G1. Stage I trains a motion tracker on about **30 minutes of Xsens boxing mocap**. Stage II distills it into a **32-dimensional latent on a unit hypersphere**. Stage III runs **Latent-Space Neural Fictitious Self-Play**, because self-play in raw joint space "often collapses" ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.16195)). In simulation, the method beat ordinary fictitious self-play 68.5% of the time and plain PPO 84.5% of the time. Its strike landing rate was 0.685, against 0.142 for raw-action self-play. Real deployment had a crutch. The robots ran ONNX policies on their onboard CPUs over ROS 2, but they located each other through **external mocap at 100 Hz**, not their own perception, and the paper validates real bouts only qualitatively ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.16195)). Learned autonomous fighting therefore exists on hardware only as a lab demo with an instrumented arena. No win rates, fall rates, or bout lengths for real robots have been published.

## One RTX 4090 now trains a boxing humanoid

The software side has become cheap. "Learning Sim-to-Real Humanoid Locomotion in 15 Minutes" trains G1 and Booster T1 walking in about **15 minutes on one RTX 4090** using FastSAC/FastTD3 with heavy domain randomization ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.01996)). MuJoCo Playground trains most of its tasks in "minutes on a single GPU" and runs entirely in Colab ([arXiv](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2502.08844)). RoboStriker's full multi-agent pipeline used one 4090 with 4,096 parallel environments, although the paper does not report total wall-clock time ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.16195)). Cloud rental is cheap too. An RTX 4090 cost about **$0.13-0.89/hour** in August-October 2026, from Vast.ai's marketplace floor up to RunPod Secure Cloud ([tech-insider](https://tech-insider.org/runpod-vs-lambda-vs-vast-ai-2026/); [Stork](https://www.stork.ai/blog/runpod-alternatives-gpu-cloud-2026)). At those rates, a 15-minute locomotion run costs pennies. A multi-day self-play run plausibly costs $10-50, though that figure is extrapolated from the hourly rates, not measured.

The training recipe has four steps. First, retarget human motion: **GMR** (MIT-licensed) takes SMPL-X, BVH, OptiTrack, or **monocular video via GVHMR**, and supports about 17 robots, including G1, Booster T1, Berkeley Humanoid Lite, and ToddlerBot ([GMR wiki](https://github.com/YanjieZe/GMR/wiki)). Phone video of yourself shadow-boxing is therefore a usable dataset. Second, train a whole-body tracker. **BeyondMimic** provides an open, reproducible mocap-to-hardware pipeline ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.08241v2)). KungfuBot/PBHC handles highly dynamic skills entirely in simulation ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.12851)). A 2026 kung fu paper adds a fall-recovery objective that markedly improves balance handling ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.13656)). Third, distill the tracker into a latent skill space. Fourth, run self-play in that latent space. Where the sim2real gap stays too large, **ASAP** fits a "delta action" model to real rollouts and fine-tunes against it, beating system identification and plain randomization ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.01143)). Its cost is a warning: motors overheated and **two G1s were damaged** during data collection ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/nvidia-researchers-demonstrate-significant-progress-in-robot-training/)).

Impacts are where simulation is weakest. The HoST get-up project found that full-mesh ankle collision models "will cause huge sim-to-real gap," and fixed it with point-contact approximations ([HoST GitHub](https://github.com/InternRobotics/HoST)). A separate get-up study argues that the usual locomotion shortcuts, such as coarse upper-body collision geometry and large timesteps, "no longer hold" once the whole body touches things ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.12152)). RoboStriker avoided simulating falls well by ending a bout whenever a non-foot body part touched the ground. It counted a hit at any contact above **10 N** and randomized contact materials, CoM, and pushes ([arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.16195)). No source quantifies how far simulated punch forces between two robots differ from real ones, and none covers simulating padding. Builders should use primitive collision shapes, randomize friction and restitution widely, and cross-check policies in a second physics engine before risking hardware.

### On Windows 11, MuJoCo Warp runs natively and the rest favors Linux

Platform choice matters for a Windows user. **MuJoCo Warp** officially supports native Windows on x86-64, with native Python (not WSL) and CUDA 12.4+ ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/mujoco-warp/)). **Isaac Lab 3.0** lists Windows 11 and now uses NVIDIA's Newton engine by default, including a MuJoCo-Warp solver preset, which makes cross-engine checks within one framework cheap. It requires Python 3.12, a Turing-or-newer GPU, and the Windows `LongPathsEnabled` registry setting before cloning ([Isaac Lab docs](https://isaac-sim.github.io/IsaacLab/main/source/isaaclab/installation.html)). Its beta documentation said Windows support "is still being completed" and recommended Ubuntu ([3.0 beta docs](https://isaac-sim.github.io/IsaacLab/v3.0.0-beta2/source/setup/installation/index.html)). Using the full Isaac Sim GUI calls for an RTX GPU with at least 16 GB VRAM and 32 GB RAM, while headless Newton does not need RTX hardware ([Isaac Lab docs](https://isaac-sim.github.io/IsaacLab/main/source/isaaclab/installation.html)). **mjlab**, the cleanest beginner framework, pairs an Isaac Lab-style API with MuJoCo Warp, installs through `uvx --from mjlab demo`, ships G1 velocity and motion-tracking tasks, and is Apache-2.0. Its README does not mention Windows ([mjlab](https://github.com/mujocolab/mjlab)). Genesis's speed claims have drawn independent criticism for contact-heavy scenes ([Stone Tao](https://stoneztao.substack.com/p/the-new-hyped-genesis-simulator-is)).

| Tool | Best for | Windows 11 path |
|---|---|---|
| MuJoCo Playground | Zero-setup first experiments; T1 get-up | Colab in browser |
| mjlab | Beginner G1 locomotion and mocap tracking | WSL2 or Linux (not documented for Windows) |
| MuJoCo Warp (raw) | Custom environments, e.g. a sumo bot | Native Windows, officially supported |
| Isaac Lab 3.0 | Reproducing RoboStriker; largest ecosystem | Listed for Windows; Ubuntu or cloud is lower risk |
| unitree_rl_gym | Reference sim2sim/deploy scripts | Legacy Isaac Gym, Linux |

## A real fight needs two robots, which doubles every price

Hardware decides what is realistic, and every price below has to be multiplied by at least two, plus spares. The budget humanoid market moved sharply in 2025-2026. **ToddlerBot** (Stanford) is the strongest fit for simulation-first work. It costs **under $6,000**, stands 0.56 m, weighs 3.4 kg, and has 30 DoF. It is built from 3D-printed parts and Dynamixel servos, and the team's per-motor system identification gave 1.3° tracking error. Its policies transferred to a second robot without re-identification. Its fall data is unusually honest: it survived **up to 7 falls before a part broke**, and a repair took about 35 minutes of printing and reassembly ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.00893v4)). A high-school curriculum built on it warns that fabrication, repairs, and mentoring push real cost above the parts list ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.25674)). **Berkeley Humanoid Lite** costs under about $5,000 for 22 joints. Each joint uses a BLDC motor with a cycloidal gearbox that prints on a 200 mm FDM printer, and its training and deployment code is open. It is a reference design you build yourself, not a product ([Berkeley Engineering](https://engineering.berkeley.edu/news/2025/11/building-bots-on-a-budget/); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.17249)). Avoid K-Scale's robots: the company shut down in November 2025 ([aiwiki](https://aiwiki.ai/wiki/k_scale_k_bot)).

Commercial options are cheaper per unit but often closed. Noetix Bumi launched at about **$1,400** ([TechNode](https://technode.com/2025/10/27/after-new-funding-noetix-robotics-explains-how-it-built-a-humanoid-robot-cheaper-than-an-iphone)). Unitree's R1 sells from about $4,370 in China and about $6,800 delivered to the US ([VnExpress](https://e.vnexpress.net/news/tech/enterprises/unitree-starts-selling-cheapest-humanoid-robot-from-6-800-5063068.html)). Reported Booster K1 prices are $4,999-5,999 ([Mike Kalil](https://mikekalil.com/blog/booster-noetix-cheap-humanoid-robots/)). The **G1 base lists at $13,500** before shipping ([theresarobotforthat](https://theresarobotforthat.com/blog/unitree-g1-price/)), though one dealer guide gives $21,600. Training your own policies needs low-level joint access, and the EDU tiers that provide it cost **$45,900-63,900**, including an "Edu Combat (Boxing)" build ([globaldronehq](https://globaldronehq.com/blogs/guides/unitree-robot-price-guide-2026)). One retail listing labels the base G1 "kein SDK/API" ("no SDK/API") ([mydealz](https://www.mydealz.de/deals/unitree-g1-humanoider-roboter-130cm-23-freiheitsgrade-ki-antrieb-3d-lidar-tiefenkamera-2h-laufzeit-kein-sdkapi-2735169)). The notes did not verify low-level access on R1, K1, or Bumi, and you should confirm it before buying any consumer humanoid. Legacy options cost more for what they do: the ROBOTIS OP3 is about $13,000 ([Funduinoshop](https://funduinoshop.com/en/robotics/robotis/robotik/robotis-op3)), and the classic ROBO-ONE kit, the Kondo KHR-3HV, is about €2,050 ([eBay.de](https://www.ebay.de/itm/225517152664)). Ready-made sim models already exist in MuJoCo Menagerie for the G1, Go2, and OP3 ([MuJoCo docs](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/3.8.0/models.html)).

Actuator choice decides how well a robot survives punches. Quasi-direct-drive BLDC joints are backdrivable, and MIT's Cheetah work showed they absorb impacts about as well as legs with series springs ([MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/119863)). Torque-controlled motors are also what simulators model directly. Small QDD units now cost about **$125-200 per joint**: the Damiao DM-J4310 from $124.99 ([rcdrone](https://rcdrone.top/collections/robotics)), the RobStride 02 (17 Nm peak) from $189 ([rcdrone](https://rcdrone.top/hi/products/robstride-02-qdd-17n-m-integrated-actuator-module-7-75-1-ratio-dual-14-bit-magnetic-encoders-48v-405g-foc-drive-for-robotics)). Smart serial servos such as the Feetech STS3215 cost about **$25** ([Core Electronics](https://core-electronics.com.au/feetech-sts3215-smart-servo.html)) but depend on position control through backlash-prone gears, which is why ToddlerBot needed careful system identification. Non-humanoid platforms are cheaper stepping stones. Stanford's Pupper v3 quadruped costs about $2,000 to build with QDD motors ([Pupper v3 docs](https://pupper-v3-documentation.readthedocs.io/en/latest/index.html)). The Unitree Go2 Air lists from $1,600 ([robozaps](https://blog.robozaps.com/b/robot-dog-cost)), though low-level control is commonly reported to be EDU-only. Insect-class combat kits cost **$278** (FingerTech Viper, 1 lb) ([RobotShop](https://www.robotshop.com/products/fingertech-viper-combat-robot-kit-w-radio-lipoly)) or **$450** (SSP 2.0, 3 lb) ([reach.dog](https://m.reach.dog/shop/just-cuz-robotics/products/ssp-robot-kit)). For onboard compute, the Jetson Orin Nano Super launched at $249 and now lists around $399 ([JetsonHacks](https://jetsonhacks.com/2026/08/26/jetsonhacks-august-2026-newsletter/)).

## Wristbands and gamepads beat the film's neural headset

The film's "think it and the bot moves" headset does not hold up against the data. Non-invasive EEG robot control tops out at roughly **15-21 bits per minute** with about **4.7 seconds per command**, even in a well-run lab study with 93% motor-imagery accuracy ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.20656)). A low-cost 16-channel system reached 78% accuracy in validation but only **62% on real robot commands** ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.08971v1)). An OpenBCI community test managed 56% with motor imagery ([OpenBCI](https://openbci.com/community/?p=5803)). That is one four-way choice every several seconds, while a fight needs a decision every fraction of a second. EEG works only as a novelty trigger, such as a slow "special move" or mode switch. The credible "neural" interface is **surface EMG at the wrist**. Meta's generic sEMG band, published in Nature in 2025, needs no per-user calibration and reaches a median **0.88 discrete gestures per second** closed-loop ([Nature PDF](https://repository.cshl.edu/id/eprint/42049/1/10.1038.s41586-025-09255-w.pdf)). Mapping forearm gestures to punch types gives the Big Hero 6 feel without the fiction.

Full-body mirroring is spectacular but slow. Measured end-to-end teleop latency ranges from about **50 ms** for ExtremControl, which skips full retargeting, to **over 700 ms** for TWIST. Most VR and RGB systems fall between 178 and 454 ms, by ExtremControl's optical-flow estimates on public videos ([ExtremControl](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11321)). TWIST's motors also overheated after 5-10 minutes of continuous use ([TWIST](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.02833v1)). Unsurprisingly, the G1's main weakness at CMG was reacting: dodging and keeping balance after being hit ([Henan gov](https://english.henan.gov.cn/2025/05-26/3162232.html)). The answer is **shared autonomy**, which REK already uses: "software handles lower-level functions such as balance while a person directs the machine" ([Runtimewire](https://runtimewire.com/article/human-vs-robot-rek-put-a-human-in-the-ring-with-a-six-foot-humanoid-robot)). Research supplies the middle layer. NVIDIA's **MaskedMimic** accepts sparse goals, such as joystick root targets, text, or VR head and hand points, through one controller ([NVIDIA blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/unified-whole-body-control-for-physically-simulated-humanoids/)). CALM lets tasks be directed at the skill level without retraining ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.02195)). RoboStriker's 32-D latent is exactly the kind of action space a gamepad could drive.

The resulting design has three layers. The pilot issues discrete moves (jab, hook, kick, block high or low, dodge) plus joystick footwork. A command-conditioned skill policy executes them. An autonomous reflex layer handles balance, protective fall poses, and getting up. Because each command selects a whole skill, even slow inputs remain usable. FightLadder's finding that two-player fighting agents stay exploitable without human demonstrations ([arXiv](https://export.arxiv.org/abs/2406.02081)) suggests recording pilot bouts and feeding them back into self-play, which is what Unitree said it planned to do ([Interesting Engineering](https://interestingengineering.com/innovation/unitree-plans-robot-boxing-match)). For scoring, combine ROBO-ONE knockdowns, where only intentional strikes count and a robot gets a 10-count to rise ([Wikipedia](https://Www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Robo_One)), with CMG-style fall penalties and sensor-verified hits. Implement the same scoring function as the simulation reward, so trained policies optimize the real rules.

## Combat-robotics safety rules transfer almost directly

The insect-class combat community has already written most of the safety rulebook. NHRL's 2026 rules require a **one-hand master power cutoff** that kills the robot within 15 seconds. Drive must stop within 30 seconds of losing signal, and each robot needs a dedicated receiver, even if it is autonomous. Batteries are capped at 75 V nominal, LiPos must be balance-charged and charging attended, and every robot must fit a fire-safety cart ([NHRL Rules](https://wiki.nhrl.io/wiki/index.php/NHRL_Rules)). SPARC recommends polycarbonate arena walls, preferably two thinner layers, at about **1/4-inch equivalent for insect classes** and 1/2 inch up to 30 lb ([SPARC v1.0](https://sparc.tools/SPARC_Arena_Construction_Best_Practices_v1.0.pdf)). NHRL even gives walkers **double the weight allowance** with pre-approval ([NHRL Rules](https://wiki.nhrl.io/wiki/index.php/NHRL_Rules)), so a small legged fighter has a legal home in existing events.

Humanoids add risks that wheeled bots lack. A mocap-mirrored G1 kicked its own operator in a viral December 2025 clip ([Interesting Engineering](https://interestingengineering.com/ai-robotics/humanoid-robot-kicks-teleoperator)), so pilots belong behind polycarbonate, outside reach. OmniH2O's authors list the lack of safety guarantees among its limitations ([OmniH2O](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.08858)), and DeepMind needed behavior regularization to keep even its small OP3s from damaging themselves ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13653)). Several safeguards follow from this evidence, though they are recommendations, not published standards. Clamp joint torque and velocity below hardware limits. Cap fist speed near the opponent. Keep rounds to 60-120 seconds to respect thermal limits. Trigger a compliant fall pose from the IMU. Wire a **radio e-stop that cuts motor power independently of the policy computer**. On regulation, the California State Athletic Commission issued a cease-and-desist after REK's September 2026 human-vs-robot bout, while **robot-vs-robot fights were unaffected** ([Runtimewire](https://runtimewire.com/article/human-vs-robot-rek-put-a-human-in-the-ring-with-a-six-foot-humanoid-robot); [Dexerto](https://www.dexerto.com/entertainment/robotics-company-served-cease-and-desist-over-human-vs-robot-fights-3416650/)). No public standard exists for humanoid punch energy or padding, so any league you run sets its own.

## A staged roadmap from $0 to a sparring pair

The practical path climbs in cost only after each stage proves the pipeline works. Every hardware tier assumes two robots, and the stage durations are estimates, not sourced figures.

| Stage | Goal | Platform | Approx. hardware cost | Exit criterion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Sim only | Learn the stack | Playground in Colab, then mjlab or MuJoCo Warp; rented 4090 at $0.13-0.89/hr | $0-50 compute | G1 or T1 walking and get-up policy trained; mocap clip tracked in sim |
| 1. Wheeled sumo | First sim2real loop with learned control | Two 1-3 lb kits ($278-450 each) with a hand-built MJCF, overhead AprilTag camera, small onboard computer | ~$600-1,300 | Policy trained in MuJoCo pushes the opponent out of the ring on real hardware; e-stop and failsafe tested |
| 2. Legged sparring | Balance under contact | Two Pupper v3 (~$2k each) or a small servo humanoid kit | ~$1,500-4,500 | Push recovery and get-up work on hardware; shared-autonomy gamepad control |
| 3. Small humanoid pair | Big Hero 6-style bouts | Two ToddlerBots (<$6k each) or Berkeley Humanoid Lites (<$5k each), padded shells, polycarbonate arena | ~$10-13k plus spares | Piloted bouts with learned strikes, reflex layer, ROBO-ONE scoring |
| 4. Research grade | Reproduce RoboStriker | Two G1 EDU ($45.9-63.9k each) plus mocap | ~$90-130k | Latent self-play policies sparring with minimal pilot input |

Stage 1 is underrated. A wheeled pusher has low-dimensional dynamics, is nearly indestructible, and exercises the entire loop: modeling, domain randomization, state estimation, and safety cutoffs. At this scale, overhead fiducials are adequate. A low-budget ArUco system tracked markers moving up to 2.2 m/s ([Technical Transactions](https://doaj.org/article/8dc7d8742a9247f19a6a9a7d3bc1475f)), fast enough for sumo but not for humanoid punches, which will blur and occlude tags. That is why RoboStriker used real mocap. In stage 3, retarget your own shadow-boxing video through GVHMR and GMR, train a tracker, and add a get-up objective before anything else, since falls dominate both event rules and real bouts. Budget for breakage: ToddlerBot's seven falls between part failures implies a 3D printer running throughout a fight night. Padding that softens impacts must also be modeled in simulation as mass, inertia, and contact parameters, or the transfer will degrade.

## Conclusion

The common framing of "can it be done in simulation" is out of date. Simulation-first training of fighting skills is solved enough to run on a gaming GPU. What remains unsolved is staying upright after being punched, which no published hardware study measures, and perceiving an opponent without external mocap. A hobbyist's advantage is scale. Small, light robots are where sim2real worked best (OP3), where falls are cheap (ToddlerBot), and where regulation is irrelevant (robot-vs-robot). The bigger, costlier humanoids that dominate the headlines are the hardest place to start.

The film got the architecture backward but the experience right. The pilot should not puppet the robot muscle by muscle. The pilot should pick intent at a few commands per second, through a gamepad or an EMG wristband, while learned reflexes do the fighting physics. Stated that way, the Big Hero 6 vision becomes an achievable systems project: a scoring function that doubles as the RL reward, a latent skill space a human can steer, and NHRL-grade cutoffs around all of it. The open questions to watch are a reconciled RoboStriker record with real-robot statistics, and the first published data on hits-to-failure for small humanoids.
